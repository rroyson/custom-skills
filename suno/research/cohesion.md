# Lyric cohesion and movement: how a song hangs together and goes somewhere

Research brief for the lyric-writing skill. Failure being fixed: verses that don't connect, images from different worlds, a chorus that never recolors, nothing changes start to end. Tags: (primary) = the writer/teacher's own book, site, interview, course; (secondary) = blog, summary, analysis.

## 1. Development / movement

**Pat Pattison (Berklee, Writing Better Lyrics)**
- Point of view first. "Who is talking? To whom? Why?" Settle it before writing verses: "figuring out your point of view can precede verse development, and indeed, in many cases, should precede setting up your boxes." Direct address is "a close-up"; first-person narrative is a "middle distance shot" and, without a reason, "well-written whining." https://www.patpattison.com/pov (primary)
- "The box": each verse+chorus is a box; the chorus is the same words but each box is "larger and heavier than the one before, because each verse adds a new angle that the chorus absorbs." Pattison's phrasing: "build your boxes so they will grow and gain weight." https://www.patpattison.com/pov (primary); https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/writing-better-lyrics-en (secondary)
- Development engine = the "six best friends" (who, what, when, where, why, how) applied to the title. Verses shift where the title is aimed; his example "Time to Let Go" = parent releasing child / child leaving / adult letting a dying parent go. Each verse "recolors" the title; the chorus is the "global" statement that absorbs what verses supply. https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas (primary); https://www.coursera.org/learn/songwriting-lyrics (primary: Module 1 "develop the point of view", "an interesting journey from start to finish")
- Second verse: "The first verse you write doesn't have to end up being a song's first verse." Ask "What if this was the second verse? Where would I have come from?" His seminar promises to "advance your ideas so the song gains rather than loses interest as it moves forward" and to solve "Second Verse Hell" via the title. https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/pat-pattison-writing-better-lyrics (primary); https://www.patpattison.com/seminars (primary)
- Sense-bound / object writing: "Treat the object as a diving board to launch you inward to the vaults of your senses." Seven senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, organic, kinesthetic). "Object writing is about showing, not telling." https://www.writersdigest.com/wd-books/writing-better-lyrics-excerpt (primary)
- Stable vs unstable (prosody): "all of the elements should support" the message. Stable = even line counts, ABAB, resolved; unstable = odd lines, ABBA, "shorten your fourth line" (Simon, "Still Crazy"). Resolution in structure should match resolution in content. https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/pat-pattison-writing-better-lyrics (primary); Coursera Module 2 "stable and unstable line/musical groupings" (primary)
- Ladder of abstraction (Hayakawa; taught by Roy Peter Clark; the same instruction as Pattison's sense-bound rule): keep most lines at the bottom rung (concrete nouns the senses can check), climb only briefly to name the abstraction. https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2004/writing-tool-13-show-and-tell/ (primary, writing teacher)

**Andrea Stolpe (Popular Lyric Writing, USC/Berklee)**
- Destination writing: "sense-bound free writing directed at a place, a person, or a time instead of an object." The camera: "The closer we zoom in, the more detail we see"; spend most of the lyric zoomed in, zoom out briefly to comment on the landscape. "Detail allows our listener to step into our shoes, know what we know, and feel how we feel." https://www.andreastolpe.com/articles/destination-writing (primary)
- Recoloration (her term): the second verse must "push the song forward or gives it more weight" so the title is re-read: "when we interpret the title in the new way as the song progresses, giving the listener a new and additional perspective." https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/simple-tools-for-writing-from-the-title/ (primary)
- Focus test: for a "clouded lyric with too many ideas," ask "what am I really trying to say?" and put the title in power positions (first/last line of chorus). https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/common-lyric-writing-roadblocks-and-ways-to-overcome-them/ (primary)
- "We should not underestimate the power of a single line or two of imagery and how it can grip the listener." https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-write-better-lyrics/ (primary)

**Sheila Davis (The Craft of Lyric Writing)** — VVTS: fix Viewpoint, Voice, Time frame, Setting early and hold them; "If anything changes within these four elements... you must make that change clear to the listener." Publisher rejection language: "It's not clear who the characters are, what's happening to whom, or when it's happening." https://archive.org/details/craftoflyricwrit0000davi (primary); https://www.simononsongs.com/p/viewpoint-voice-time-frame-and-setting (secondary)

**Jimmy Webb (Tunesmith)** — open with a question or ambiguity, don't reveal the situation too soon; let the listener ask "where are we going?" "By the Time I Get to Phoenix" = three verses, three places, one continuous drive: time and place are the plot. https://archive.org/details/tunesmith00jimm (primary); https://sobrief.com/books/tunesmith (secondary)

**Jeff Tweedy (How to Write One Song)** — words as sound first, meaning later; the "word ladder" (10 verbs from a profession x 10 nouns from the room) to make images you couldn't plan; "Company in My Back" written from an insect's view because "the language was making him see things." Cohesion can come from a consistent seeing, not a plot. https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/670253/how-to-write-one-song-by-jeff-tweedy/ (primary); https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/jeff-tweedy-songwriting-book-excerpt-1070889/ (primary excerpt)

**Writers on how a song moves**
- Isbell: "In a song, you have such a little amount of space to work with. And it's really hard to tell a story unless it's just the right, most pertinent details." On "Elephant": "I didn't want to write about cancer as much as the relationship... there's not a whole lot that can be said about cancer. But there are infinite details about human relationships." https://grantland.com/hollywood-prospectus/the-most-gut-wrenching-jason-isbell-songs-with-guest-commentary-by-jason-isbell/ (primary). Songs "start off very literal" then "develop... more meanings"; first edit pass: "try to find clichés." https://www.musicradar.com/news/classic-interview-jason-isbell-offers-his-9-tips-for-songwriters (primary)
- Prine: starts from one picture and lets the character speak. "I had this really vivid picture of this woman standing over the dishwater with soap in her hands, and just walking away from it all." Give "what color the ashtray is," leave the feeling unnamed. https://americansongwriter.com/john-prine-angel-from-montgomery-behind-the-song/ (primary quotes). "Hello in There" began as "hollering through a hollow log, trying to get through to somebody." https://www.pressherald.com/2020/04/08/angel-from-montgomery-the-story-of-john-prines-most-unforgettable-song/ (secondary)
- Lucinda Williams: her father (poet Miller Williams) as "b.s. detector": don't reuse "angel" in "Lake Charles" after "Drunken Angel." One image, one job. https://americansongwriter.com/the-lucinda-williams-lyric-that-detailed-her-travel-filled-youth/ (secondary)
- Springsteen: Born to Run conceived as vignettes following one character through a day; "Thunder Road" is "an invitation," porch to road in one night. https://americansongwriter.com/behind-the-song-thunder-road/ (secondary); https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/bruce-springsteen-thunder-road-book-814981/ (primary, paywalled)
- Paul Simon: "Maybe I'm supposed to go on a trip and see what I'm writing about, and I did." Graceland's spine is a literal drive (Route 61) the associations hang from. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/paul-simon/graceland (primary quotes)
- Craig Finn: "if you put it on a corner you've walked by 4,000 times, maybe that story rings a little more true"; characters "maybe they'd appear in a few songs." https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2024/11/11/craig-finn-plays-songs-and-talks-about-songwriting (primary)
- Jason Molina: recurring images are deliberate infrastructure. Trees "provide a visual continuity between places"; "mountains, valleys, rivers, shores, horizons, are somehow always a boundary"; "I don't try to entertain or to tell direct stories in these songs. I am just meditating." https://drownedinsound.com/news/4136068-dis-meets-jason-molina-magnolia-electric-co-songs--ohia (primary)
- Elliott Smith: "if there's a story it's usually a fragmented one"; a song is "like describing a dream last night but you're having it now when you're awake." https://www.nme.com/blogs/nme-blogs/elliott-smith-archive-interview-there-has-to-be-darkness-in-my-songs-782397 (primary)
- Bridgers on "Kyoto": lyrics are "a collage chopped up into one experience" — but the one experience (impostor syndrome, her father) is fixed: "This song is about impostor syndrome... feeling like I'm living someone else's life." https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BUQzAHuJHKk (primary, NYT Diary of a Song); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_(Phoebe_Bridgers_song) (secondary)
- Julien Baker: "meticulously specific"; songs are "communication" not "exposition." https://atwoodmagazine.com/jblo-julien-baker-interview-2021-music-little-oblivions-feature/ (primary)
- Justin Vernon on "Holocene": "the different verses are really about Eau Claire and about my people from there"; the song is "special and not special at the same time." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_(song) (secondary, quoting Vernon)

**Nashville rules**
- Second verse: "To write a second verse, ask yourself, 'What else happened?' Or, 'Then what happened?'" — Jason Blume. https://www.bmi.com/advice/to_bridge_or_not_to_bridge_and_other_songwriting_questions1 (primary)
- Move the camera: take "your songwriter's camera to a new angle" so verse 2 is the first chance to "shed a new light on the chorus." https://flypaper.soundfly.com/write/songwriting-school-verse-two-now-what/ (secondary); Goldmacher: don't restate verse 1 "only using different words." https://www.cliffgoldmacher.com/a-few-tips-on-writing-your-second-verse/ (primary)
- Bridge changes the angle or the time: "Ideally, in this section a new lyric angle, new perspective, and/or new information is introduced." (Blume, above). NSAI's anti-pattern: "Here's a minor chord... and some yada, yada information to stall until you can get back to the chorus." https://www.nashvillesongwriters.com/anatomy-great-lyric (primary)
- Last chorus means something different: change one word in the final chorus; withhold the title until the end so it becomes a payoff. https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/take-your-songwriting-to-the-next-level-by-adding-a-twist (primary)
- Ralph Murphy: "No unexplained details. No speed bumps."; "you will always write the second verse first." https://www.billboard.com/music/music-news/ascap-expo-ralph-murphys-laws-of-songwriting-1559086/ (primary)
- "Furniture": tangible objects (chair, car, food, clothes) that "show us as opposed to telling us" and build "a word landscape that is cinematic and immediately recognizable" (credited to Phil Swann / Harriet Schock). The working rule: describe what happens in the room, not the room. https://www.tanyawinch.com/lyrical-furniture/ (secondary); Schock: "the power and logic of reality to keep the song on course." https://www.amazon.com/Becoming-Remarkable-Songwriters-Those-Love/dp/157733034X (primary)

## 2. Cohesion tools, literal and non-literal

- **Controlling image / conceit / image system.** McKee: "An image system is a strategy of motifs, a category of imagery embedded in the film that repeats in sight and sound from beginning to end with persistence and great variation." Pick one category (a car, weather, drink, a house) and draw every image from it. https://quotefancy.com/quote/2192865/Robert-McKee-An-image-system-is-a-strategy-of-motifs-a-category-of-imagery-embedded-in (secondary quoting Story). Pattison on metaphor: "a collision between ideas that don't belong together"; verbs are "the power amplifiers of language." https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips (primary)
- **Image family / semantic field.** Robin Frederick: "The best images are the ones that don't travel alone"; "Be on the lookout for images with associations that conflict with your theme or with each other." https://robinfrederick.com/five-essential-songwriting-tips/tip-2-use-a-family-of-images/ (primary)
- **Objective correlative.** Eliot: "a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events which shall be the formula for that particular emotion." The object carries the feeling so the feeling is never named. https://www.poetryfoundation.org/education/glossary/objective-correlative (primary); songwriting example, Sting's bottle = isolation: https://songwriterschopshop.com/blog/how-to-not-write-depressing-song-lyrics/ (secondary)
- **Motif and callback.** An object returns changed (Fast Car's car; Molina's trees/boundaries; Finn's recurring characters). https://www.pastemagazine.com/music/the-hold-steady/the-hold-steady-craig-finns-characters-are-growing (secondary)
- **Through-line / spine sentence.** Stolpe's "what am I really trying to say?"; Goldmacher's "Is everything related to the hook/message?" and "Each line furthers the story." https://www.cliffgoldmacher.com/better-lyrics/ (primary)
- **Refrain that recolors.** Same words, new meaning because the verse before it moved (Pattison's boxes; Stolpe's recoloration; Pattison's "productive repetition": "By simply repeating a portion of the line, we move from a declarative sentence into a question, creating new energy, and adding a new idea.") https://www.patpattison.com/productive-repetition (primary)
- **Framing device.** Letter (Famous Blue Raincoat, signed "Sincerely, L. Cohen"; Christmas Card), dinner table (Billie Joe), bookend line. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/leonard-cohen/famous-blue-raincoat (secondary)
- **Turn / volta.** A rhetorical shift ("but", "yet") that reverses or reframes; in songs, the bridge or last verse is the volta slot. https://poets.org/glossary/volta (primary)
- **Withheld fact / theory of omission.** Hemingway: "The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water." Omit only what you know. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_the_Afternoon (primary quote). Gentry: what went off the bridge is "irrelevant"; the song is "a study in unconscious cruelty." https://americansongwriter.com/bobbie-gentrys-ode-to-billie-joe-isnt-about-what-you-think-it-is/ (secondary quoting Gentry)
- **Time as structure.** One night (Thunder Road), one drive (Phoenix, Graceland), then/now (The River), years in verses (Fast Car: verses present tense, chorus past tense). https://devantaylorpublications.medium.com/verse-by-verse-tracy-chapman-fast-car-4133b6fbc388 (secondary)
- **Place as structure.** Davis's Setting; Stolpe's destination writing; Vernon's verses each a different Eau Claire memory.
- **List song.** Coheres by one rule the list obeys: items "relate to—and lead the listener to—the title," with the last item emphasized or the opposite of the title. https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/how-to-use-the-list-technique-in-your-song (primary); items must come "from the same unifying principle." https://www.simononsongs.com/p/how-to-write-a-list-song (secondary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_song (secondary)
- **Associative cohesion (when there is no story).** What stays constant: a stable speaker and addressee (Dylan's Louise/Johanna, the room, the night), a recurring image system (light/electricity/ghosts across every verse of "Visions"), one register of diction, one syntactic habit (Not's anaphora "not / nor"), a refrain that returns, sound patterning. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/bob-dylan/visions-of-johanna (secondary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_(song) (secondary; Boilen: "the inability to describe what that something is, only to be able to explain what it is not"). Yorke's cut-ups still obey a fixed tone and speaker. https://www.openculture.com/2015/02/bowie-cut-up-technique.html (secondary)
- **Negative-space cohesion.** The thing never said is the same thing in every verse: cancer named once as a joke in "Elephant," the bridge object in "Billie Joe," the betrayal in "Pancho and Lefty" (Van Zandt: "there's nothing in that song that says Pancho and Lefty ever knew each other"). https://americansongwriter.com/townes-van-zandt-pancho-and-lefty/ (primary quote)

## 3. Tests on a finished lyric

- Spine: write the song as one sentence (who / wants / obstacle / change). If you can't, there is no spine. (Stolpe, Goldmacher Q1, above.)
- Noun census: list every concrete noun; count the worlds they come from. More than two worlds without a bridge image = mood board. (Frederick, McKee.)
- Delta: what is different between V1 and last verse — time, place, knowledge, decision, distance? Name at least one. (Pattison boxes; Blume "then what happened?")
- Shuffle: swap V1 and V2. If nothing breaks, nothing moved. (Goldmacher "have you already said it?")
- Recolor: read the chorus after V1, then after V2. Different reading? (Stolpe recoloration.)
- Last line vs first line: does the last line know something the first didn't?
- Image audit: for each image, say which job it does for the spine. Cut the ones with no job. (Isbell "most pertinent details.")
- VVTS: viewpoint, voice, tense, setting held — or any change signaled? (Davis.)
- Omission check: is the withheld thing withheld on purpose and felt in every verse (Hemingway), or just missing (Murphy "unexplained details")?
- Bridge check: does it add angle, time, or information, or restate? (Blume, NSAI.)

## 4. Worked examples

- Fast Car (Chapman): the car is the spine object; verses in present tense at four life stages, chorus in past tense; car goes from escape to stagnation. https://devantaylorpublications.medium.com/verse-by-verse-tracy-chapman-fast-car-4133b6fbc388 (secondary)
- Brandy (Looking Glass): harbor-town frame; the sailor's line "my life, my love and my lady is the sea" recolors the chorus's "what a good wife you would be" from compliment to sentence. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandy_(You're_a_Fine_Girl) (secondary)
- Pancho and Lefty: "you" verse, Pancho verse, Lefty verse, federales refrain; the connection between the two is withheld. https://americansongwriter.com/townes-van-zandt-pancho-and-lefty/ (primary quote)
- Ode to Billie Joe: dinner table as frame; peas and pie carry the indifference; the object thrown off the bridge is never named. https://americansongwriter.com/bobbie-gentrys-ode-to-billie-joe-isnt-about-what-you-think-it-is/ (secondary)
- The River: then/now; the river is the same place that meant freedom and now memory; built from his brother-in-law's life. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/bruce-springsteen/the-river (secondary)
- Hello in There / Angel from Montgomery (Prine): one image (dishwater) generates the character; details given, feelings withheld. (Section 1 URLs)
- A Case of You (Mitchell): one conceit, drink/wine/blood, carried through map, bar, and "holy wine." https://americansongwriter.com/behind-the-meaning-of-joni-mitchells-case-of-you/ (secondary)
- Thunder Road: one night, one porch to one road; screen door opens, car leaves; "an invitation." (Section 1 URLs)
- Graceland: associative images (National guitar, "human trampoline") hung on a literal drive with a fixed companion (the boy). https://www.songfacts.com/facts/paul-simon/graceland (primary quotes)
- Elephant (Isbell): the relationship, not the disease; "cancer" only in her joke; the title image lands in the last line. https://grantland.com/hollywood-prospectus/the-most-gut-wrenching-jason-isbell-songs-with-guest-commentary-by-jason-isbell/ (primary)
- Cover Me Up: verses = past (drinking, the road), chorus = present declaration; time is the structure. https://americansongwriter.com/jason-isbell-cover-me-up/ (secondary)
- Visions of Johanna: no plot; cohesion from one night, one room, one speaker, Louise present / Johanna absent, and the light/electricity image system. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/bob-dylan/visions-of-johanna (secondary)
- Kyoto / Motion Sickness (Bridgers): collage of real specifics (payphone, construction, the British accent) locked to one addressee and one feeling. https://learningmusicskills.com/phoebe-bridgers-songwriting-analysis/ (secondary); NYT Diary of a Song (primary, above)
- Not (Big Thief): list by negation; every line obeys one syntactic rule; the thing itself never named. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_(song) (secondary)
- Holocene / Re: Stacks (Bon Iver): three separate memories held by one refrain ("I could see for miles") and one place; Re: Stacks uses Qumran/excavation as a controlling metaphor for digging out a past. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_(song) (secondary); https://lentsongs.substack.com/p/re-stacks-by-bon-iver (secondary)
- Hold Steady (Finn): characters recur across songs and albums; a fixed cast and fixed streets do the cohering. (Section 1 URLs)
- Christmas Card from a Hooker in Minneapolis (Waits): letter frame; every hopeful detail is reversed by the last verse (no husband, "parole come Valentine's Day"). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Card_from_a_Hooker_in_Minneapolis (secondary)
- Famous Blue Raincoat (Cohen): letter frame with date, weather, address, signature; the raincoat is the one object that carries the whole triangle. https://www.songfacts.com/facts/leonard-cohen/famous-blue-raincoat (secondary)

## 5. Failure modes named by teachers

- "Second Verse Hell" (Pattison, lyricworkroom): nothing left to say because V1 said everything and the title has no DNA left. https://www.patpattison.com/seminars (primary); https://lyricworkroom.com/second-verse-hell/ (secondary)
- The restated verse: "simply restating the same thing you've said in your first verse only using different words" (Goldmacher, primary).
- The stalling bridge: "yada, yada information to stall until you can get back to the chorus" (NSAI, primary).
- The clouded lyric: "too many ideas / lacking focus" (Stolpe, primary).
- Unexplained details / speed bumps (Murphy, primary): details that don't belong to the spine.
- Well-written whining (Pattison, primary): first-person feeling with no addressee or reason.
- Cliché / prefabricated language (Pattison, Sound on Sound, primary): the "documentary puppy" falls asleep.
- Conflicting image associations (Frederick, primary) — our label "mood board": pretty images from different worlds.
- Laundry list with no rule (Wikipedia list song; Blume list technique) — our label "montage": images with no through-line, verses shuffle-able.
- Telling language / "greeting card": the feeling named instead of shown (Stolpe's showing vs telling; Pattison "showing, not telling"). Labels "greeting card," "montage," "mood board" are this report's shorthand for the sourced faults above.

## Decision rules

1. Before verse 1, answer "Who is talking? To whom? Why?" and hold it (Pattison POV; Davis VVTS).
2. Write the spine sentence first; every line must serve it (Stolpe "what am I really trying to say?"; Goldmacher Q1).
3. Pick one image system (a category: car, kitchen, weather, hospital) and draw every concrete noun from it (McKee; Frederick).
4. Choose one object to carry the feeling and never name the feeling (Eliot's objective correlative; Prine's ashtray).
5. Stay on the bottom rung: sense-bound nouns and verbs; climb to the abstraction once, in the chorus, if at all (Pattison; Clark).
6. Verse 2 must change one of: time, place, knowledge, decision, distance; ask "then what happened?" or "what happened before?" (Blume; Pattison).
7. Verse 2 moves the camera; it never re-describes verse 1 (Goldmacher; Flypaper).
8. Write the chorus so verse 2 recolors it; if the chorus reads the same after V2, rewrite V2 (Stolpe recoloration; Pattison boxes).
9. The bridge adds a new angle, time, or fact, or is cut (Blume; NSAI).
10. Change one word or withhold the title for the last chorus so the end knows more than the start (Blume twist).
11. Fix viewpoint, voice, tense, and setting; if one changes, mark the change in the line (Davis).
12. Furniture over rooms: show what happens with the objects, not the objects (Winch/Schock).
13. Withhold on purpose: decide the one fact never said and make every verse feel its weight (Hemingway; Gentry; Van Zandt).
14. In a non-narrative lyric, keep constant at least three of: speaker, addressee, place/night, image system, refrain, syntax rule, diction register (Dylan; Lenker; Molina).
15. If it is a list, state the rule the list obeys and end on the item that pays off the title (Blume; Hawkins).
16. Match structure to content: unstable line groups and rhyme for unresolved feeling, stable for resolved (Pattison prosody).
17. Only "the right, most pertinent details"; cut any image without a job for the spine (Isbell; Murphy).
18. One image, one job; don't reuse a key image in a different sense (Miller Williams to Lucinda).

## Cohesion tests

1. Can you say the song in one sentence with a change in it (who / wants / what shifts)?
2. Do all concrete nouns come from one or two worlds, with any second world bridged by an image?
3. Is at least one of time / place / knowledge / decision / distance different between verse 1 and the last verse?
4. If you swap verse 1 and verse 2, does something break?
5. Does the chorus read differently after verse 2 than after verse 1?
6. Does the last line know something the first line didn't?
7. Can you name the job each image does for the spine?
8. Are viewpoint, voice, tense, and setting held — or is each change signaled in the line?
9. Is the withheld fact the same in every verse and felt, not merely absent?
10. Does the bridge add angle, time, or information rather than restate?
